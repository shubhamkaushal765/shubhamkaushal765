# Evidence for brand-intuition mechanisms

**Tiers:** Robust (replicated, many studies), Supported (good evidence, narrower or mixed), Working model (useful framing, thin or context-bound evidence), Contested (serious disagreement). Entries state the claim, tier, key sources and limits. If a detail here is not in the cited source, it was dropped rather than guessed.

## Dual-process (System 1 / System 2)

- **Claim:** judgement runs on fast, automatic processes and slow, effortful ones; fast ones often drive first impressions.
- **Tier:** Supported as a simplification. "System 1 buys, System 2 justifies" is a Working model, not a finding.
- **Sources:** Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011); the dual-process literature it summarises (Stanovich and West, Evans).
- **Limits:** two "systems" are a metaphor; many processes sit between. Chapter 4 social-priming studies failed to replicate and Kahneman acknowledged this. Considered B2B purchases weight System 2 more. No credible source supports the unsourced "ninety-five percent subconscious" figure often credited to Zaltman.

## Mere exposure

- **Claim:** repeated exposure to a neutral stimulus raises liking.
- **Tier:** Robust.
- **Sources:** Zajonc 1968; Bornstein 1989 meta-analysis.
- **Limits:** the effect weakens or reverses with too many exposures (boredom, wear-out); stronger for brief or unrecognised exposures; it does not rescue a disliked or poor product.

## Processing fluency

- **Claim:** stimuli that are easy to process feel more familiar, true and pleasant.
- **Tier:** Supported, with variable effect sizes.
- **Sources:** Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman 2004 (review); Alter and Oppenheimer 2006 (shares with easier-to-pronounce ticker names were associated with better short-term returns).
- **Limits:** effects are small and context dependent; difficulty can sometimes trigger deeper thought; not evidence that plain names always win, and the stock-name effect fades over longer horizons. Replicability of individual fluency studies varies.

## Halo effect and aesthetic-usability

- **Claim:** a positive impression on one trait spills over to others; attractive interfaces are perceived as more usable.
- **Tier:** Robust (halo), Supported (aesthetic-usability).
- **Sources:** Thorndike 1920 (halo); Dion, Berscheid and Walster 1972 (attractiveness stereotype); Kurosu and Kashimura 1995 (apparent usability vs inherent usability; 26 ATM layouts, Japan; replicated by Tractinsky 1997 in Israel).
- **Limits:** the halo can invert when real use contradicts it; perceived usability does not fix actual usability problems. Usability itself belongs to [ui-ux-review](https://github.com/shubhamkaushal765/NotableNotes/blob/master/.claude/skills/ui-ux-review/SKILL.md).

## Social proof

- **Claim:** people use others' choices as evidence of what is right, especially under uncertainty.
- **Tier:** Robust.
- **Sources:** Asch 1951 (conformity); Cialdini, Reno and Kallgren 1990 (descriptive norms, littering); Salganik, Dodds and Watts 2006 (artificial music market, *Science*); Cialdini, *Influence* (1984).
- **Limits:** some lab effects shrink in field settings; effect depends on similarity to the observer and plausibility of the proof. Fabricated proof is unethical and, for reviews, unlawful in the EU (see Ethics line in the skill).

## Somatic markers

- **Claim:** bodily emotional signals, learned from past outcomes, bias decisions toward good options.
- **Tier:** Contested.
- **Sources:** Damasio, *Descartes' Error* (1994); critique in Dunn, Dalgleish and Lawrence 2006.
- **Limits:** the evidence base (including the Iowa Gambling Task interpretation) is disputed. Use only as a metaphor for "past experience leaves a feeling that guides choice". Do not build claims on it.

## Price-quality heuristic

- **Claim:** higher price is read as higher quality, and can alter experienced quality.
- **Tier:** Supported.
- **Sources:** Rao and Monroe 1989 (*Journal of Marketing Research*, meta-analysis of price, brand name and store name effects on perceived quality; a positive but modest price-quality link); Plassmann et al. 2008 (identical wine labelled with higher prices was rated as more pleasant, with differing activity in a reward-related brain region).
- **Limits:** small sample, wine specific, a lab setting; the heuristic is stronger when quality is hard to judge and weaker with experts. Treat Plassmann (small sample) as illustrative, not proof.

## Associative memory

- **Claim:** brands are retrieved through a network of linked cues (category, situation, feeling).
- **Tier:** Working model.
- **Sources:** Keller 1993 (customer-based brand equity, *Journal of Marketing*).
- **Limits:** a conceptual framework more than a tested law; link the brand to buying cues and measure recall.

## Anchoring and decoy

- **Claim:** an initial number frames later estimates; adding a dominated option shifts choice toward the option that dominates it.
- **Tier:** Anchoring Supported; decoy Contested.
- **Sources:** Tversky and Kahneman 1974 (anchoring); Huber, Payne and Puto 1982 (asymmetric dominance, decoy); Frederick, Lee and Baskin 2014 (*Journal of Marketing Research*, attraction effect largely absent with experienced or perceptual attributes) and the original authors' reply in the same issue.
- **Limits:** field effects are smaller and context dependent. Easy to turn manipulative (see Ethics line).

## Judgement heuristics (recognition, availability, affect, default)

- **Claim:** people judge by what is recognised, easily recalled, how it feels, and what is preset. Maps common brand claims such as "familiar means safe" (recognition, mere exposure), "popular means good" (social proof) and "expensive means better" (price-quality).
- **Tier:** Supported overall; recognition heuristic is Contested.
- **Sources:** Tversky and Kahneman 1973 (availability); Goldstein and Gigerenzer 2002 (recognition heuristic; its scope is debated); Slovic et al. 2002 and 2007 (affect heuristic); Johnson and Goldstein 2003 (defaults, organ donation, *Science*).
- **Limits:** heuristics are context dependent; default effects depend on switching cost and visibility.

## Typeface personality

- **Claim:** typefaces carry tone or personality.
- **Tier:** Working model.
- **Sources:** none cited; evidence is limited and mixed.
- **Limits:** use as a tiebreaker; legibility is not a working model and comes first.

## Shape symbolism (bouba-kiki)

- **Claim:** people tend to match rounded shapes with soft-sounding words and angular shapes with sharp-sounding words.
- **Tier:** Supported.
- **Sources:** Köhler 1929; Ramachandran and Hubbard 2001.
- **Limits:** demonstrates a sound-shape association, not that round logos make a product friendly. Evidence for brand effects is thinner and the effect is modest and varies by language and culture.

## Sound symbolism in brand names

- **Claim:** sounds in a name carry meaning (for example, front vowels suggest small, light, fast; back vowels suggest large, heavy).
- **Tier:** Working model for naming practice; the underlying association is Supported.
- **Sources:** Klink 2000; Yorkston and Menon 2004.
- **Limits:** lab tasks with invented names; effects are modest and language specific. Use as a tiebreaker and test in the target language.

## Colour

- **Claim:** colours carry associations that can shape brand impressions.
- **Tier:** Working model.
- **Sources:** Labrecque and Milne 2012 (colour and brand personality).
- **Limits:** associations are mostly context, category and culture dependent. "Blue is trust, red is urgency, green is health" are tendencies in some markets, not laws. Category fit and contrast usually matter more; recommend testing over asserting.

## Brand personality and self-congruity

- **Claim:** people ascribe human traits to brands (sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, ruggedness), and prefer brands that match their self-image.
- **Tier:** Working model (personality dimensions); Supported (self-congruity).
- **Sources:** Aaker 1997 (US-derived five dimensions); Aaker, Benet-Martinez and Garolera 2001 (dimensions differ in Japan and Spain); Austin, Siguaw and Mattila 2003 (generalisability critique); Sirgy 1982 (self-congruity).
- **Limits:** the structure was derived in one culture and does not replicate cleanly elsewhere; the scale describes perception, not a lever you pull. Use as vocabulary for tone, not a measurement.

## Distinctiveness, mental and physical availability

- **Claim:** brands grow mainly through reach, being easy to think of and easy to buy; distinctive assets aid recognition.
- **Tier:** Supported.
- **Sources:** Sharp, *How Brands Grow* (2010); Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (2018).
- **Limits:** the evidence base centres on repeat-purchase consumer categories; applicability to B2B and infrequent purchases is less established. Sharp's distinctiveness-over-differentiation stance is contested by position-based branding. Both are presented in the skill with when each fits.

## Narrative transportation

- **Claim:** being absorbed into a story changes beliefs and attitudes in line with the story.
- **Tier:** Supported.
- **Sources:** Green and Brock 2000.
- **Limits:** depends on the story's quality and the audience's absorption; effects are on attitudes, not necessarily behaviour. The claim that stories are twenty-odd times more memorable is an unsourced statistic, often misattributed to Jerome Bruner, and must not be repeated.

## Books and reliability notes

- **Kahneman 2011:** priming studies in chapter 4 failed to replicate; Kahneman acknowledged it.
- **Ariely 2008:** a 2012 PNAS honesty study he co-authored was retracted in 2021 after the field data were shown to be invalid (who was responsible is disputed); the sign-at-the-top effect also failed a large replication (Kristal et al. 2020, PNAS). Prefer findings independently replicated.
- **Cialdini 1984:** durable framework; some lab effects are smaller in the field.
- **Sharp 2010:** evidence-led, repeat-purchase categories.
- **Eyal 2014:** practitioner framework, not primary research; apply his manipulation matrix (would you use it yourself, does it improve users' lives).
