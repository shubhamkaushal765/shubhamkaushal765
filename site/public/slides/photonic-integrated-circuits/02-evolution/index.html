Photonic integrated circuits: the technology evolution

Sixty years from the first planar waveguide to silicon photonics you can buy, and the two demands (communication and compute) that pulled it along.

Arrow keys to move. f fullscreen. ? key map.

[ 01 ]|why a technology evolves

Science runs on curiosity. Technology runs on a need.

Nobody builds a technology and then waits for an application. There is always a push, and the technology takes the shape of that push.

For photonic integrated circuits the push came from two directions: moving data between places (communication) and moving data inside a processor (compute). This deck follows the key events first, then the two demands.

[ 02 ]|the key events

Six decades, in the order the pieces arrived

[ 03 ]|confinement

Planar means confined in one direction. A real circuit needs two.

A planar (slab) waveguide is a guiding layer between two lower-index layers, symmetric or asymmetric. Light cannot escape vertically, but it spreads freely sideways.

A 3-D waveguide confines in x and y and propagates along z. The optical fiber does this with a round core; the channel waveguide does it with a rectangular one. Same idea as the fiber, on a flat substrate.

This is the whole concept of a photonic integrated circuit: confine light into a small space where you can act on it.

[ 04 ]|the 1980s: lithium niobate

Confinement is for interaction, and lithium niobate proved it first

Why guide light at all? To increase light-matter interaction, and to enable electro-optic and nonlinear effects.

Lithium niobate was the known platform, and by the 1980s its waveguides existed: a titanium strip diffused into the crystal makes a buried channel of slightly higher index.

The disruption: light was no longer handled only with lenses, prisms, and mirrors. On chip you could guide it, shift its phase, and set its polarisation.

Both concepts and first demonstrations arrived in the late 80s and early 90s. Then the race to build components and circuits for a real market began.

[ 06 ]|the 1990s: III-V

III-V reached the market ten years after its first demonstration

A direct band gap made generation and detection easy. Guiding light from one point of the chip to another was already understood. So the pieces just had to be put together.

Propagation loss was high in both platforms. In III-V it did not matter much, because the same direct gap gives you an amplifier on chip.

Source, detector, amplifier, modulator: every discrete function a complete circuit needs was available in one material.

[ 07 ]|the 1990s: silicon

Silicon had the fab but not the physics, and the timeline stretched

The idea was "the CMOS technology is there, let us use it". Photonics did not cooperate.

  • Scattering loss. Silicon is transparent at 1300 and 1550 nm, yet losses were high. The reason is the structure, not the material: high confinement pushes the field onto four interfaces, and the silicon-to-oxide index contrast is large. Scattering grows as Δn to the fourth power.
  • Contamination. Metal or carbon in the oxide or on the surface absorbs light. Microelectronics tolerates it; a waveguide does not.
  • No source, no detector. Guiding existed, but indirect-gap silicon could not emit, and detection was not there either.

[ 08 ]|2000 to 2010: integration on silicon

Silicon borrowed the functions it could not make

  • Detector: germanium, monolithic. SiGe and Ge were already in CMOS for strain engineering, so Ge detectors came almost for free.
  • Source, attempt one: band-engineered Ge. Dope germanium and pull one indirect valley up; GeSn showed light generation from a photonic IC.
  • Source, attempt two: III-V on top. Bond a direct-gap III-V layer onto silicon. This heterogeneous integration gave the fully integrated silicon photonic IC.

From here the route went commercial. Silicon photonic products are on the market now.

[ 10 ]|demand one: communication

The internet outgrew copper, and fiber took over the transport

Around the turn of the century, processing went from isolated stations to distributed, and the connections were coaxial copper. It held for a while, then had to give way to optical fiber.

Fiber enabled resource distribution (the video you watch streams from servers you never locate) and performance (live streams with no noticeable delay). Access reached tens of Gbps to the home.

Wireless did not change this. 4G and 5G promise multi-gigabit per user, and every such network is backed by fiber. Fiber transports; where signals are split and combined at the aggregate level, that is a photonic IC.

[ 11 ]|why optical wins

Two numbers justify the move: loss and carrier frequency

Loss. High-frequency electrical signals attenuate in cables and in air. Past 1 GHz you are looking at tens of dB; even at tens of MHz it is a few dB per kilometre, and communication spans cities and continents. Light in a dielectric does not rely on a conductor, so loss can be far lower.

Bandwidth. The engineer's thumb rule: usable bandwidth is about 10 % of the carrier. A 100 MHz carrier gives 10 MHz. A 100 THz carrier gives 10 THz, which was unheard of in the copper days and more than the traffic could fill.

[ 12 ]|demand two: compute

Transistor scaling ran into the same copper wall, one centimetre wide

Shrinking the node (90 nm to 45 nm) shrinks the footprint per function, so the free space fills with more cores: a single core at 130 nm became 16 cores at 32 nm. A thousand cores is easy.

The problem is not making cores. They must talk to each other, and to memory on and off the chip, over copper interconnects. Past a few tens of GHz the loss is prohibitive. This is the bandwidth bottleneck.

The solution is already known from telecom. Take the thousands-of-kilometres fiber network and scale it down to a centimetre. That is not just shrinking; the physics changes, which is exactly why photonic ICs are interesting.

[ 14 ]|what confinement buys you

Small volume means low voltage, high speed, and strong interaction

  • Electromagnetic optics, not rays. The structures are smaller than the wavelength, so ray optics fails. Maxwell's solutions of the waveguide are the guided modes, and they carry the energy.
  • Volts, not kilovolts. Light confined in a small volume needs a small field to steer it. Small voltage swings fast, the material and the light both react quickly, so the device runs at high speed.
  • High power density. Sub-wavelength confinement raises the optical intensity, which opens linear and nonlinear optics: new wavelengths, optical signal processing.

[ 16 ]|check your understanding

Three questions before the next lecture

  1. III-V and silicon were proposed at nearly the same time. III-V shipped products in ten years and silicon took two decades. Name the physical reason and the process reason for the gap.
  2. Silicon is transparent at 1550 nm, yet early silicon waveguides were lossy. Where did the loss come from, and why does a high index contrast make it worse?
  3. A processor with a thousand cores is easy to make. Why does it not simply run a thousand times faster, and what does the telecom industry's history suggest as the fix?
what to remember Technology follows demand. Communication pushed optics into fiber because a dielectric carrier at hundreds of THz beats copper on both loss and bandwidth; compute is now pushing the same fiber network down to a centimetre. Integration wins because confinement buys low voltage, high speed, strong interaction, and alignment that is fixed at fabrication.
