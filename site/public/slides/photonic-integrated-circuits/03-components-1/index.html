Photonic integrated circuit components, part 1: power handling

The passive toolkit: waveguides and bends, Y-branch and multimode-interference splitters, mirrors and gratings, and the directional coupler.

Everything a circuit does falls into three kinds of function

[ 02 ]|interconnect

The simplest component: get light from A to B, straight or bent

A straight waveguide is the planar version of a free-space beam between two points.

Unlike free space, a guided wave lets you bend. S-bends and full curves steer light around other structures on the chip. Keep the bend radius above the platform's limit and the light arrives with no loss.

Too tight a bend and the mode leaks out of the outer wall; the limit depends on the index contrast.

[ 03 ]|power splitting

One input, two outputs: the beam splitter becomes a Y-branch

In bulk optics a beam splitter sends intensity I out as I/2 and I/2. It works, but it is a large element.

In guided-wave optics, branch the waveguide like a Y. Think of a fork in a road: light arrives at the junction and a short taper lets it divide into two propagating modes, one per arm.

The Y-branch is the direct translation of the beam splitter onto a chip.

[ 04 ]|multimode interference

Or let a slab do the splitting: multimode interference

Same goal, different mechanism. Insert a short, wide slab section between the input and the two outputs.

The wide slab supports many modes. The input excites several of them at once, and as they travel they interfere.

At the right length the interference pattern forms an image of the input at two spots. Put the output waveguides there and you have a 50:50 splitter. The equations come later in the course; this is the picture.

[ 05 ]|ratio and direction

Any ratio you design for, and it works backwards as a combiner

50:50 is not a rule. The way the modes are made to interfere fixes the ratio, so 80:20 or 90:10 is a design choice.

Splitter and combiner are the same device. Reverse the direction of energy flow and the outputs become inputs. Y-splitter, Y-combiner, MMI splitter, MMI combiner: reciprocal devices.

50 : 50

[ 06 ]|more outputs

1 x 4: the Y-branch stacks stages, the MMI does it in one device

A Y-junction is inherently 1 x 2. For four outputs you cascade: one 1 x 2 feeding two more, so three splitters in two stages, and 50:50 becomes 25:25:25:25.

An MMI is different. Change the slab's length and width and the interference forms four images instead of two. One device, 1 x 4 directly, and the same idea scales to 1 x N.

Y-branches: rudimentary and stackable. MMIs: scalable by design.

[ 07 ]|reflection

Send light back: a metal mirror, or a stack of index steps

A mirror orthogonal to the beam works on chip as it does on the table: end the waveguide in metal, and the strong reflection returns the light down the same guide.

A grating does it without metal. Alternate two indices n1, n2, n1, n2 along the propagation direction z. Each interface transmits a little and reflects a little.

If the reflections from every interface arrive in phase, they add up to a strong effective reflection; the transmissions add up to what gets through. That condition depends on n1, n2, d1, d2, and on wavelength: a grating is a mirror for chosen wavelengths.

[ 08 ]|reflection, on chip

You do not need two materials: corrugate the thickness instead

All a grating needs is a periodic index difference along z. A waveguide's effective index depends on its thickness, so etching the silicon thick, thin, thick, thin does the same job as stacking n1 and n2.

Light entering the corrugated section is reflected back down the guide. Mirror at the end, or grating in the guide: both are on-chip reflectors.

[ 09 ]|directional coupler

Two inputs, two outputs: bring two guides close and light crosses over

Ports A and B on the left, C and D on the right. Light from A can go to D, from B to C, all of it or a chosen fraction, in one device.

The mechanism is crosstalk. Two waveguides close together leak into each other, and the coupling region uses that leak on purpose. The gap needed depends on the index contrast.

"Directional" means light couples forward only, never back toward the input. The device is still reciprocal, so it is not an isolator.

[ 10 ]|directional coupler

How much crosses over is set by the length of the coupling region

Coupled power is a cosine-like function of length. At one length everything has crossed to the other guide; at twice that it has all come back; in between, a fraction.

Pick the length where the crossing is 0.5 and you have built a 1 x 2 power splitter out of two waveguides. Design it right and you can also reach exactly zero.

0.50

[ 11 ]|directional coupler

Its best trick: tap one percent to watch a circuit without disturbing it

An electrical signal can be probed almost anywhere. Light cannot be tapped without changing what the device does.

A directional coupler designed for a tiny fraction, say 1 %, pulls off just enough light to monitor what is happening inside, and the rest continues unaffected.

The same small coupling feeds light into a cavity and reads it back out, which is where ring resonators come from later in the course.

[ 13 ]|check your understanding

Three questions before the next lecture

  1. You need a 1 x 8 equal splitter. How many Y-branches does it take, and how many MMIs? What changes in the MMI design between 1 x 2 and 1 x 8?
  2. A metal mirror reflects every wavelength; a grating reflects only some. Explain the difference in terms of what the reflected waves from successive interfaces have to do.
  3. A directional coupler transfers all the light at length Lc. What does it do at 2 Lc, and at Lc / 2? Why does that make it the natural device for a 1 % monitor tap?
what to remember Power handling is passive and reciprocal. Guide and bend, split with a Y-branch or self-image in an MMI, reflect with a mirror or a wavelength-selective grating, and cross light between guides with a directional coupler whose length sets the fraction.
